\begin{abstract}
% =============================================================================
We study environment-assisted quantum transport (ENAQT) in tight-binding chains
with site-local pure dephasing, an irreversible trap and uniform excitation
loss, computing the trapping yield $\eta(\gp)$ exactly within the Lindblad
model from a single linear solve per parameter point.
For a two-site donor--acceptor pair the dephasing enhancement
$\enh$ grows with the energy bias, from $1.06$ at $\varepsilon=0.5\Delta$
to $7.20$ at $\varepsilon=5\Delta$, with the optimal dephasing rate tracking
the bias, $\gps\approx\varepsilon$.
For linear energy funnels of $N=2$--$20$ sites the optimal rate falls as
$\gps\propto N^{-1.20}$ while the peak yield falls from $0.80$ to $0.25$.
The enhancement ratio itself is dominated by its reference point: measured
against $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$ it grows linearly up to $N=10$ and plateaus near
$38$, whereas against the coherent limit $\gp=0$ it keeps growing to
$1.3\times10^{3}$ at $N=20$.
In an ensemble of 1{,}000 chains with Gaussian site disorder
($\sigma=2\Delta$, $N=2$--$15$), $97.4\%$ of realizations show an interior
yield maximum with at least $1\%$ gain.
Median enhancement ratios exceed those of the ordered funnel at large $N$ only
because disorder suppresses the coherent baseline; the absolute peak yield of
disordered chains is lower at every $N$.
Optimizing site energies for the largest ratio drives the chain to a two-level
step profile whose peak yield is again below that of the funnel.
For the seven-site FMO Hamiltonian with the trap at BChl~3 we find a
$4.7$-fold enhancement at $\gps=1.9\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$, of the same order as a
high-temperature estimate of the protein dephasing rate.
We conclude that peak yield and optimal dephasing rate, not the enhancement
ratio, are the robust figures of merit for noise-assisted transport.
\end{abstract}

\section{\label{sec:intro}Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Environment-assisted quantum transport (ENAQT), also called dephasing-assisted
or noise-assisted transport, is the observation that moderate environmental
dephasing can increase the probability that an excitation reaches a trap in a
network of coupled sites~\cite{mohseni2008,plenio2008,rebentrost2009,caruso2009}.
At weak dephasing, transport is limited by coherent effects: energy mismatch
between sites, destructive interference and Anderson
localization~\cite{anderson1958}.
At strong dephasing, frequent effective measurement of site populations
freezes the dynamics (a quantum Zeno effect).
In between, dephasing broadens site energies enough to bridge mismatches
without freezing the motion, and the trapping yield $\eta(\gp)$ has an
interior maximum.

The effect has been analyzed in light-harvesting models such as the
Fenna--Matthews--Olson (FMO) complex~\cite{mohseni2008,plenio2008,rebentrost2009,caruso2009,wu2010,moix2011},
in ordered chains~\cite{kassal2012}, in disordered
chains~\cite{znidaric2013,moix2013,novo2016,zerahharush2020} and
networks~\cite{walschaers2013,zerahharush2018,coates2023}, in tilted
(Wannier--Stark) chains~\cite{jacob2024} and in strongly correlated boundary-driven
systems~\cite{yeler2026}, and it has been observed in a ten-qubit trapped-ion
network~\cite{maier2019}.
Coates, Lovett and Gauger showed that disordered networks can have more than
one optimal dephasing regime~\cite{coates2023}; Zerah-Harush and Dubi traced
ENAQT to a competition between dephasing-driven population equalization and the
source--sink gradient~\cite{zerahharush2018} and studied how disorder and
interactions modify it~\cite{zerahharush2020}; Jacob \textit{et al.}\ showed
that in a boundary-driven tilted chain the current peaks when the dephasing rate
matches the Bloch-oscillation frequency~\cite{jacob2024}.

A quantity that recurs throughout this literature, and in our own earlier
drafts, is the enhancement ratio $\enh$: the peak yield divided by the yield at
vanishing dephasing.
The present paper makes three points about it, using a minimal and fully
reproducible Lindblad model.
(i)~For linear energy funnels we give length-scaling relations for the optimal
dephasing rate and the peak yield, and we show that the apparent saturation of
the enhancement ratio with $N$ is an artefact of evaluating $\etaz$ at small
but finite dephasing.
(ii)~For random site disorder we give ensemble statistics of the ratio, the
peak yield and the baseline separately, and show that large ratios in
disordered chains come from a collapsing coherent baseline rather than from
better noise-assisted transport.
(iii)~Optimizing site energies for the ratio produces a step-like landscape
that is worse than a smooth funnel in absolute yield.
We close with a seven-site FMO benchmark.

% =============================================================================
\section{\label{sec:model}Model and method}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Master equation}

For $N$ sites $\ket{j}$, $j=1,\ldots,N$, with Hamiltonian $H$, the density
matrix obeys
\begin{equation}
  \dot\rho = -i[H,\rho]
    + \gp\sum_j\!\left(P_j\rho P_j - \half\{P_j,\rho\}\right)
    - \frac{\kappa}{2}\{P_N,\rho\} - \Gamma\rho ,
  \label{eq:lindblad}
\end{equation}
with $P_j=\ket{j}\bra{j}$ and $\hbar=1$.
The second term is site-local pure dephasing, which damps every coherence
$\rho_{mn}$ ($m\neq n$) at rate $\gp$ and leaves populations unchanged.
The third term is irreversible trapping from site $N$ at rate $\kappa$, and the
last term is a uniform loss (recombination or fluorescence) at rate $\Gamma$.
The excitation starts on site 1, $\rho(0)=P_1$.

\subsection{Trapping yield}

The yield is the probability that the excitation is trapped before it is lost,
\begin{equation}
  \eta = \kappa\int_0^\infty \rho_{NN}(t)\,dt .
  \label{eq:yield_def}
\end{equation}
With column-stacked $\mathrm{vec}(\rho)$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:lindblad}) reads
$\partial_t\mathrm{vec}(\rho)=\Lop\,\mathrm{vec}(\rho)$ with
\begin{align}
  \Lop =\;& -i\left(\mathbf{1}\otimes H - H^{T}\otimes\mathbf{1}\right)
    + \gp\sum_j\left(P_j\otimes P_j - \half\mathbf{1}\otimes P_j
      - \half P_j\otimes\mathbf{1}\right) \notag\\
    & - \frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\mathbf{1}\otimes P_N + P_N\otimes\mathbf{1}\right)
      - \Gamma\,\mathbf{1}_{N^2}.
  \label{eq:liouvillian}
\end{align}
For $\Gamma>0$ every eigenvalue of $\Lop$ has a negative real part, so
\begin{equation}
  \eta = \kappa\left[-\Lop^{-1}\mathrm{vec}(\rho(0))\right]_{NN},
  \label{eq:yield_solve}
\end{equation}
one $N^2\times N^2$ linear solve per parameter point.
Writing $\Lop=\Lop_\text{base}+\gp\Lop_\text{deph}$, a sweep over $\gp$
reuses both matrices.
All results except the site-energy optimization of Sec.~\ref{sec:optimal} take under a minute on one CPU core.

\subsection{Hamiltonians and parameters}

\textit{Chains.}
Nearest-neighbour coupling $\Delta=1$ sets the energy and inverse-time unit.
The linear energy funnel has site energies
$\varepsilon_j=\varepsilon_\text{tot}/2-(j-1)\varepsilon_\text{tot}/(N-1)$
with $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=5\Delta$, so the total donor--trap bias is fixed
as $N$ grows.
Disordered chains draw $\varepsilon_j\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$
independently.

\textit{Two-site model.}
For $N=2$ we use the spin-boson convention
$H=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\sigma_z+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\sigma_x$, i.e.\ an
inter-site coupling of $\Delta/2$; this is the convention used in
Sec.~\ref{sec:two_site} and Fig.~\ref{fig:two_site}.
The $N=2$ funnel of Sec.~\ref{sec:funnel} uses coupling $\Delta$ and therefore
gives a different enhancement ($2.75$ rather than $7.20$ at
$\varepsilon=5\Delta$).

\textit{Rates.}
Unless stated otherwise, $\kappa=0.1\Delta$ and $\Gamma=0.01\Delta$.
One-dimensional sweeps use log-spaced $\gp\in[10^{-3},10^{3}]\Delta$
(300 points for ordered chains, 100 points for disorder ensembles).

\textit{Enhancement ratio.}
We define $\etap=\max_{\gp}\eta(\gp)$ on the grid and $\gps$ its location.
Following our earlier scripts, $\etaz$ denotes the yield at the lowest grid
point, $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$, unless explicitly marked as the true coherent
limit $\eta(\gp=0)$.
Section~\ref{sec:funnel} shows that this choice matters.
A realization is counted as showing ENAQT when $\gps$ is an interior grid
point and $\enh>1.01$.

\section{\label{sec:results}Results}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{\label{sec:two_site}Two sites: the role of bias and trap rate}

Figure~\ref{fig:two_site}(a) and Table~\ref{tab:two_site} show the two-site
yield.
Without bias the yield decreases monotonically with $\gp$: coherent transfer
between degenerate sites is already efficient and dephasing only slows it.
With bias, the coherent yield drops (the excitation oscillates with reduced
amplitude $\propto\Delta^2/(\Delta^2+\varepsilon^2)$) while dephasing-assisted
hopping restores it, and the interior maximum sits at
$\gps\approx\varepsilon$.
This is the familiar resonance condition: dephasing broadens each level by
$\sim\gp$, and transfer is fastest when the broadening matches the
mismatch~\cite{rebentrost2009,jacob2024}.
At very strong dephasing all curves collapse onto the Zeno tail,
$\eta(10^{3}\Delta)=0.043$.

large.
The peak-to-Zeno ratio varies much less ($12.6$--$17.4$).

\subsection{\label{sec:funnel}Linear energy funnels}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Figure~\ref{fig:funnel} and Table~\ref{tab:funnel} summarize funnels of
$N=2$--$20$ sites at fixed total bias $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=5\Delta$.
Every funnel shows an interior yield maximum.
Two quantities behave simply with length.
The optimal dephasing rate follows
\begin{equation}
  \gps \approx 9.2\,\Delta\;N^{-1.20}\qquad (N=2\text{--}20,\;R^2=0.992),
  \label{eq:gp_scaling}
\end{equation}
consistent with the resonance picture of Sec.~\ref{sec:two_site}: the
site-to-site mismatch is $\varepsilon_\text{tot}/(N-1)$, and $\gps$ follows it
with a somewhat steeper exponent than the $N^{-1}$ that mismatch alone would
give.
The peak yield falls steadily, from $0.80$ at $N=2$ to $0.25$ at $N=20$,
because the transit time grows with $N$ while the loss rate $\Gamma$ is fixed.

The enhancement ratio does not have a unique scaling law.
Measured against $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$ it grows almost linearly for $N\le10$,
\begin{equation}
  \enh \approx 3.85\,N - 4.9\qquad (N\le 10,\;R^2=0.996),
\end{equation}
then levels off at $36$--$38$ for $N=12$--$20$.
Measured against the true coherent limit, the same data give
$\eta_\text{peak}/\eta(0)=107$ at $N=10$, $407$ at $N=15$ and $1.3\times10^{3}$
at $N=20$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:funnel}(b)).
The plateau therefore does not reflect a limit on noise-assisted transport;
it appears because, in long funnels, even $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$ already lifts the
yield well above its coherent value (by a factor of $43$ at $N=15$).
The ratio is controlled by how small the baseline is, and the baseline depends
on where it is evaluated.

\textit{Flat chains.}
With all site energies equal ($N=3,7,15$), the yield decreases monotonically
with $\gp$ and no ENAQT occurs, in agreement with the result that
dephasing cannot enhance end-to-end transport in a uniform
chain~\cite{kassal2012}.

\subsection{\label{sec:disorder}Random site disorder}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

We drew 100 disorder realizations (seeds $0$--$99$) with $\sigma=2\Delta$ for
each $N\in\{2,3,4,5,6,7,8,10,12,15\}$, 1{,}000 chains in total.
Table~\ref{tab:disorder} and Fig.~\ref{fig:disorder} give the statistics.

\textit{Prevalence.}
An interior maximum with at least $1\%$ gain occurs in 974 of the 1{,}000
chains.
The fraction is lowest for the shortest chains ($89\%$ at $N=2$, $92\%$ at
$N=3$), where a realization can have a small bias and behave like the
degenerate case of Sec.~\ref{sec:two_site}, and is $98$--$100\%$ for
$N\ge4$.
So ENAQT is the typical behaviour of a disordered chain with a trap, not a
fine-tuned one, in line with earlier
studies~\cite{moix2013,walschaers2013,novo2016,zerahharush2020}.

\textit{Ratio versus yield.}
The median ratio grows quickly with $N$ and exceeds the ordered funnel's ratio
(against $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$) from $N=10$ on: $35.7$ versus $32.4$ at $N=10$,
and $244$ versus $37.9$ at $N=15$.
The distribution is strongly right-skewed (mean $6.9\times10^{3}$ at $N=15$,
maximum $2.4\times10^{5}$).
Figure~\ref{fig:disorder}(b) shows where the large ratios come from: the median
peak yield decreases gently with $N$, from $0.82$ to $0.13$, while the median
baseline collapses from $0.66$ to $5.5\times10^{-4}$, as expected once the
localization length becomes shorter than the chain.
The disordered chains do not transport better than the funnel.
Their median peak yield is below the funnel's at every $N$ ($0.43$ versus
$0.52$ at $N=7$; $0.13$ versus $0.32$ at $N=15$), and measured against the true
coherent limit the funnel's ratio is also the larger one ($107$ versus a median
of $41$ at $N=10$, $407$ versus $354$ at $N=15$).

\textit{Disorder strength.}
At $N=7$ (50 realizations per $\sigma$) the median ratio is $1.00$ at
$\sigma=0.25\Delta$, $1.74$ at $\sigma=\Delta$, $8.3$ at $2\Delta$, $97$ at
$3\Delta$ and $7.2\times10^{3}$ at $5\Delta$, while the median peak yield
falls from $0.60$ to $0.26$.
For $\sigma\ge\Delta$ the median ratio is well described by
$\ln(\enh)\approx2.15\,\sigma/\Delta-1.83$ ($R^2=0.996$;
Fig.~\ref{fig:disorder}(c)).
We do not attach a mechanism to this exponential form: it describes the range
studied, and the baseline it reflects is set jointly by localization, the
trap and the loss rate.
(In one dimension the localization length at weak disorder scales as
$\xi\propto\Delta^2/\sigma^2$; for $\sigma\gtrsim\Delta$ it is of the order of
a few sites, so the chains of Fig.~\ref{fig:disorder}(c) are strongly
localized.)

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\label{sec:optimal}Optimizing the ratio}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

To see what the ratio rewards, we maximized $\enh$ over the site energies
$\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_{N-1}\in[-5\Delta,5\Delta]$ with
$\varepsilon_N=0$, using differential evolution
(\texttt{scipy.optimize.differential\_evolution}, population $8(N-1)$,
100 generations, L-BFGS-B polish), with $\etap$ taken over 30 log-spaced points
in $[10^{-3},10^{2}]\Delta$ and $\etaz=\eta(10^{-8}\Delta)$.

For $N\le10$ the optimizer converges to a two-level step profile at the bounds:
the first half of the chain at $+5\Delta$, the second half at $-5\Delta$,
and the trap at $0$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:optimal}(a)); for $N=12$ and $15$ it returns
irregular profiles that are not converged.
These landscapes achieve enormous ratios ($87$ at $N=3$, $9.0\times10^{3}$ at
$N=5$, $8.0\times10^{5}$ at $N=7$) by making coherent transfer almost
impossible: $\etaz$ is $7\times10^{-3}$ at $N=3$ and $4\times10^{-7}$ at $N=7$.
Their peak yields are, however, lower than those of a funnel with
$\varepsilon_j=(N-j)\Delta$ at every $N$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:optimal}(b)):
$0.65$ versus $0.75$ at $N=3$, $0.32$ versus $0.50$ at $N=7$, and $0.21$
versus $0.37$ at $N=10$.
For $N\ge10$ both baselines fall below $10^{-9}$, where the ratios are no longer
meaningful in double precision.
Maximizing the enhancement ratio thus selects for a poor coherent baseline, not
for good transport, and it should not be used as a design objective.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\label{sec:fmo}FMO benchmark}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

For the seven-site FMO Hamiltonian of Adolphs and Renger~\cite{adolphs2006}
(site energies and couplings in cm$^{-1}$, expressed in units of
$\Delta_\text{FMO}=87.7\,\text{cm}^{-1}$, the BChl~1--2 coupling), with the
excitation starting on BChl~1 and the trap on BChl~3, the site coupled to the
reaction center~\cite{adolphs2006,rebentrost2009}, we find
\begin{equation*}
  \gps = 1.87\,\Delta_\text{FMO},\qquad
  \etap = 0.46,\qquad
  \eta(0)=0.098,\qquad
  \etap/\eta(0) = 4.7 .
\end{equation*}
In time units, $\Delta_\text{FMO}^{-1}=60.5$\,fs.
For comparison, the high-temperature pure-dephasing rate
$2\lambda k_BT/\hbar\gamma_c$ for the Drude bath parameters of Ishizaki and
Fleming~\cite{ishizaki2009} ($\lambda=35\,\text{cm}^{-1}$,
$\gamma_c^{-1}=50$\,fs, $T=300$\,K) is $1.57\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$, of the same
order as $\gps$.
This is consistent with earlier conclusions that FMO operates near the ENAQT
optimum~\cite{rebentrost2009,caruso2009}, with the caveat that our model uses
the same trap and loss rates as the chains above rather than fitted
biological values.
An earlier version of this work placed the trap on BChl~7; that choice gives a
much larger ratio ($32.2$ at $\gps=1.55\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$) because BChl~7 is
weakly coupled to BChl~1, which illustrates again how strongly the ratio
depends on the coherent baseline.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\label{sec:2d}Square lattices}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

As a first look beyond one dimension we computed $L\times L$ square lattices
($L=2$--$6$) with uniform coupling $\Delta$, a diagonal energy gradient from
$5\Delta$ at the source corner to $0$ at the trap corner, and the same rates
(80 log-spaced $\gp$ points in $[10^{-3},10^{2}]\Delta$,
$\etaz=\eta(10^{-8}\Delta)$).
Table~\ref{tab:2d} lists the results.
The enhancement stays modest ($3.1$--$4.7$), the peak yield falls with lattice
size as in 1D, and $\gps$ decreases with $L$.
For disordered lattices ($\sigma=2\Delta$, 50 realizations per $L$, 50
$\gp$ points) $76$--$96\%$ of realizations show $\enh>1.01$.
Larger ensembles and other geometries are needed before drawing conclusions
about dimensionality.

\section{\label{sec:discussion}Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\textit{What scales simply.}
In ordered funnels the optimal dephasing rate tracks the site-to-site energy
mismatch, $\gps\propto N^{-1.20}$ at fixed total bias, and the peak yield
decreases smoothly with length.
Both are insensitive to how the coherent limit is treated, and both are
directly comparable with experiment, where the yield and the dephasing rate are
the measurable quantities~\cite{maier2019}.

\textit{What does not.}
The enhancement ratio mixes two very different quantities: how well
dephasing-assisted transport works, and how badly coherent transport fails.
In long or disordered chains the second dominates.
As a result the ratio (i) has no unique length dependence, since it can
plateau or grow by orders of magnitude depending on the reference rate;
(ii)~is largest for disordered chains whose absolute yield is worse; and
(iii)~when optimized, selects energy landscapes that block coherent transfer
rather than ones that transport well.
We therefore recommend reporting $\etap$ and $\gps$, with the ratio given
only together with the exact reference point used for $\etaz$.

\textit{Relation to earlier work.}
The resonance condition $\gps\sim$ (energy mismatch) agrees with two-site
analyses~\cite{rebentrost2009} and with the Bloch-frequency matching found for
tilted chains~\cite{jacob2024}.
The near-universal presence of ENAQT in disordered chains agrees with
Refs.~\cite{moix2013,walschaers2013,novo2016,zerahharush2020}; our ensemble
adds length-resolved statistics with the peak yield and baseline separated.

\textit{Limitations.}
The model is Markovian with uniform, uncorrelated pure dephasing; it neglects
non-Markovian bath memory, which matters for FMO on sub-picosecond
timescales~\cite{ishizaki2009,moix2011}, and thermal relaxation toward lower
sites, which a Haken--Strobl--Reineker dephasing term cannot describe.
The trap and loss rates are fixed rather than fitted to a specific system.
The disorder and 2D ensembles are moderate in size (100 and 50 realizations).
Appendix~\ref{app:heom} explains why we do not use the QD3SET-1 spin-boson data
as a validation of the trapping model.

% =============================================================================
\section{\label{sec:conclusion}Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

Within a minimal Lindblad model of a dephased chain with a trap:
\begin{enumerate}
\item ENAQT requires an energy mismatch; the optimal dephasing rate tracks
  it ($\gps\approx\varepsilon$ for two sites, $\gps\propto N^{-1.20}$ for
  funnels of fixed total bias).
\item ENAQT occurs in $97.4\%$ of 1{,}000 disordered chains
  ($\sigma=2\Delta$, $N=2$--$15$).
\item The enhancement ratio is dominated by the coherent baseline: its
  apparent saturation in funnels, its large values in disordered chains and
  the step-like landscapes that maximize it all reflect a vanishing
  baseline, not better transport.
\item For FMO with the trap on BChl~3 the optimal dephasing rate,
  $1.9\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$, is of the order of a standard estimate of the
  protein dephasing rate.
\end{enumerate}

\section{\label{app:heom}Remarks on the QD3SET-1 spin-boson data}

An earlier version of this work compared the trapping model with the 1{,}000
numerically exact HEOM spin-boson trajectories of the QD3SET-1
dataset~\cite{ullah2023} ($\lambda=0.1$--$1.0\,\Delta$,
$\gamma_c=1$--$10\,\Delta$, $\beta=0.1$--$1.0\,\Delta^{-1}$,
$\varepsilon\in\{0,\Delta\}$, $\rho_{11}(0)=1$, $t\le20\,\Delta^{-1}$).
Binning all trajectories by the high-temperature estimate
$\gp=2\lambda/(\beta\gamma_c)$ gives a non-monotonic mean of
$\rho_{22}(t=20\,\Delta^{-1})$ for $\varepsilon=\Delta$, with a maximum of
$0.771$ at $\gp\approx0.13\Delta$ against $0.608$ at the lowest bin.
We no longer treat this as evidence for ENAQT or as a validation of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lindblad}), for three reasons.
First, there is no trap: $\rho_{22}$ at a fixed time measures relaxation toward
thermal equilibrium, not a trapping yield.
Second, the binned curve pools trajectories with different temperatures and
reorganization energies; within single-temperature slices we find no interior
maximum, so the pooled shape reflects how the parameter grid maps onto $\gp$.
Third, the HEOM bath includes thermal relaxation and memory that the pure
dephasing model lacks.
A quantitative test of the trapping model against exact dynamics would need
HEOM (or similar) simulations that include the trap, which we leave for future
work.
