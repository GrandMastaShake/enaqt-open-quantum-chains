\begin{abstract}
% =============================================================================
We study environment-assisted quantum transport (ENAQT) in tight-binding chains
with site-local pure dephasing, an irreversible trap and uniform excitation
loss, and compute the trapping yield $\eta(\gp)$ exactly from one linear
solve per parameter point.
For linear energy funnels of $N=2$--$20$ sites at fixed total bias, the optimal
dephasing rate equals the nearest-neighbour energy mismatch,
$\gps=\varepsilon_\text{tot}/(N-1)$, to within $2\%$, the trapped-chain
counterpart of the rule found for boundary-driven tilted chains; it maximizes
the Haken--Strobl--Reineker hopping rate on every bond at once and holds to
within $15\%$ over two decades of trap and loss rates.
Because the dephasing acts at infinite temperature, the yield is exactly
invariant under reversing the bias, and a flat chain, which shows no ENAQT, reaches a higher yield
than any funnel: dephasing only repairs yield lost to energy mismatch.
The enhancement ratio $\enh$ is set by its reference point and by the rates:
for $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=5\Delta$, above the bandwidth, it plateaus at
$36$--$38$ against $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$ but reaches $1.3\times10^{3}$ at $N=20$
against the coherent limit, whereas for $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=2\Delta$ it
stays near $1.6$.
In 1{,}000 chains with Gaussian site disorder ($\sigma=2\Delta$), $97.4\%$ show
ENAQT and median ratios exceed the funnel's because the coherent baseline
collapses; only 4 of these chains beat the flat chain, while weaker disorder of
matched nearest-neighbour strength beats the funnel.
Maximizing the ratio over site energies has no finite optimum: it drives the
profile to the bounds of the search space and lowers the yield.
For the seven-site FMO complex with the trap at BChl~3,
$\gps=1.9\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$, of the order of a high-temperature estimate
of the protein dephasing rate, while the $4.7$-fold enhancement
falls to $1.14$ at biological trap and loss rates.
Peak yield and optimal dephasing rate, not the enhancement ratio, are the
robust figures of merit.
\end{abstract}

\section{\label{sec:intro}Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Environment-assisted quantum transport (ENAQT), also called dephasing-assisted
or noise-assisted transport, is the observation that moderate environmental
dephasing can increase the probability that an excitation reaches a trap in a
network of coupled sites~\cite{mohseni2008,plenio2008,rebentrost2009,caruso2009}.
At weak dephasing, transport is limited by coherent effects: energy mismatch
between sites, destructive interference and Anderson
localization~\cite{anderson1958}.
At strong dephasing, frequent effective measurement of site populations
freezes the dynamics (a quantum Zeno effect).
In between, dephasing broadens site energies enough to bridge mismatches
without freezing the motion, and the trapping yield $\eta(\gp)$ has an
interior maximum.
In the incoherent limit this is the classic Haken--Strobl--Reineker (HSR)
picture of exciton hopping in a dephasing
environment~\cite{haken1972,haken1973,moix2013}.

The effect has been analyzed in light-harvesting models such as the
Fenna--Matthews--Olson (FMO) complex~\cite{mohseni2008,plenio2008,rebentrost2009,caruso2009,wu2010,moix2011},
in ordered systems~\cite{kassal2012}, in disordered
chains~\cite{znidaric2013,moix2013,novo2016,zhang2017,zerahharush2020,coates2021}
and networks~\cite{zerahharush2018,coates2023}, in tilted (Wannier--Stark)
chains~\cite{burkhardt2013,jacob2024}, in lossy
lattices~\cite{skalkin2025} and in strongly correlated boundary-driven
systems~\cite{yeler2026}.
It has been observed in an optical cavity network~\cite{viciani2015}, a
photonic waveguide network~\cite{biggerstaff2016}, superconducting
circuits~\cite{potocnik2018}, a ten-qubit trapped-ion
network~\cite{maier2019} and a photonic simulation of the FMO
complex~\cite{tang2024}.
Coates, Lovett and Gauger showed that disordered networks can have more than
one optimal dephasing regime~\cite{coates2023}; Zerah-Harush and Dubi traced
ENAQT to a competition between dephasing-driven population equalization and the
source--sink gradient~\cite{zerahharush2018} and studied how disorder and
interactions modify it~\cite{zerahharush2020}; Jacob \textit{et al.}\ showed
that in a boundary-driven tilted chain the current peaks when the dephasing rate
equals the nearest-neighbour energy difference, the Bloch-oscillation
frequency, so that at fixed total tilt the optimum falls as $1/L$ with chain
length~\cite{jacob2024}.
Length laws for the optimal dephasing rate have also been found in disordered
chains with a sink~\cite{zhang2017}, where it is tied to eigenstate
localization~\cite{coates2021}, and in lossy lattices~\cite{skalkin2025}.
Recent work optimizes site energies~\cite{lawrence2026} and site-dependent
dephasing~\cite{lawrence2026b} for the absolute steady-state flux, and an
evidence review of quantum effects in biology treats noise-assisted
transport as a conditional design principle, to be tested by dephasing
perturbations linked to functional yield~\cite{gassab2026}.

A quantity that recurs throughout this literature, and in our own earlier
drafts, is the enhancement ratio $\enh$: the peak yield divided by a reference
yield at weak or vanishing dephasing.
That such enhancements depend on the comparison regime has been noted
before~\cite{kassal2013}, very large ratios have been reported where the
undephased transport is already tiny~\cite{dwiputra2020}, and at realistic
parameters the gain in photosynthetic complexes appears to be
small~\cite{zerahharush2021}.
Using a minimal and fully reproducible Lindblad model, we separate what is
robust from what is not.
(i)~In linear energy funnels the optimum of the trapping yield is fixed by the
nearest-neighbour mismatch, $\gps=\varepsilon_\text{tot}/(N-1)$, the HSR
resonance condition applied to every bond and the trapped-chain counterpart of
the boundary-driven result of Ref.~\cite{jacob2024}; this rule survives large
changes of the trap and loss rates.
(ii)~The yield is exactly symmetric under reversal of the bias, and the flat
chain outperforms every funnel; dephasing only recovers what the mismatch
costs.
(iii)~The enhancement ratio has no unique length dependence: its apparent
saturation in funnels depends on the reference rate, the total bias and the
loss rate, and its large values in disordered chains, or in landscapes
optimized for it, come from a collapsing coherent baseline rather than from
better transport.
We close with a seven-site FMO benchmark, including biological trap and loss
rates, and a first look at square lattices.

% =============================================================================
\section{\label{sec:model}Model and method}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Master equation}

For $N$ sites $\ket{j}$, $j=1,\ldots,N$, with Hamiltonian $H$, the density
matrix obeys
\begin{equation}
  \dot\rho = -i[H,\rho]
    + \gp\sum_j\!\left(P_j\rho P_j - \half\{P_j,\rho\}\right)
    - \frac{\kappa}{2}\{P_N,\rho\} - \Gamma\rho ,
  \label{eq:lindblad}
\end{equation}
with $P_j=\ket{j}\bra{j}$ and $\hbar=1$.
The second term is site-local pure dephasing, which damps every coherence
$\rho_{mn}$ ($m\neq n$) at rate $\gp$ and leaves populations unchanged; it is
the HSR model at infinite temperature~\cite{haken1972,haken1973}.
The third term is irreversible trapping from site $N$ at rate $\kappa$, and the
last term is a uniform loss (recombination or fluorescence) at rate $\Gamma$.
The excitation starts on site 1, $\rho(0)=P_1$.

\subsection{Trapping yield}

The yield is the probability that the excitation is trapped before it is lost,
\begin{equation}
  \eta = \kappa\int_0^\infty \rho_{NN}(t)\,dt .
  \label{eq:yield_def}
\end{equation}
With column-stacked $\mathrm{vec}(\rho)$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:lindblad}) reads
$\partial_t\mathrm{vec}(\rho)=\Lop\,\mathrm{vec}(\rho)$ with
\begin{align}
  \Lop =\;& -i\left(\mathbf{1}\otimes H - H^{T}\otimes\mathbf{1}\right)
    + \gp\sum_j\left(P_j\otimes P_j - \half\mathbf{1}\otimes P_j
      - \half P_j\otimes\mathbf{1}\right) \notag\\
    & - \frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\mathbf{1}\otimes P_N + P_N\otimes\mathbf{1}\right)
      - \Gamma\,\mathbf{1}_{N^2}.
  \label{eq:liouvillian}
\end{align}
For $\Gamma>0$ every eigenvalue of $\Lop$ has a negative real part, so
\begin{equation}
  \eta = \kappa\left[-\Lop^{-1}\mathrm{vec}(\rho(0))\right]_{NN},
  \label{eq:yield_solve}
\end{equation}
one $N^2\times N^2$ linear solve per parameter point.
Writing $\Lop=\Lop_\text{base}+\gp\Lop_\text{deph}$, a sweep over $\gp$
reuses both matrices.

\subsection{\label{sec:symmetry}Two exact properties}

\textit{Bias reversal.}
For a real Hamiltonian on a bipartite lattice (a chain or a square lattice),
the yield is invariant under $\varepsilon_j\to-\varepsilon_j$ for all sites at
once.
Complex conjugation of Eq.~(\ref{eq:lindblad}) maps the evolution under $H$
onto the evolution under $-H$, since the dephasing, trap and loss terms are
real, and the gauge transformation $\ket{j}\to(-1)^j\ket{j}$ restores the sign
of the couplings.
Populations, and hence $\eta$, are unchanged by both operations.
An uphill funnel and the corresponding downhill funnel therefore have
identical yields at every $\gp$: infinite-temperature dephasing provides no
downhill drift.
The analogous statement for the boundary-driven current of a tilted chain is
noted in Refs.~\cite{jacob2024,mendozaarenas2024}, and under HSR dephasing
forward and backward hopping rates are equal~\cite{moix2013}.

\textit{Incoherent hopping rate.}
For two sites with mismatch $\dE$ and coupling $J$, adiabatic elimination of
the coherence in Eq.~(\ref{eq:lindblad}) gives the HSR
rate~\cite{haken1973,moix2013}
\begin{equation}
  k(\gp) = \frac{2J^2\gp}{\gp^2+\dE^2},
  \label{eq:hsr_rate}
\end{equation}
symmetric in $\dE$ and maximal at $\gp=|\dE|$, where $k=J^2/|\dE|$.
The elimination is controlled for $J\ll|\gp+i\dE|$; we use
Eq.~(\ref{eq:hsr_rate}) only to interpret the exact results.

\subsection{Hamiltonians and parameters}

\textit{Chains.}
Nearest-neighbour coupling $\Delta=1$ sets the energy and inverse-time unit
(bandwidth $4\Delta$).
The linear energy funnel has site energies
$\varepsilon_j=\varepsilon_\text{tot}/2-(j-1)\varepsilon_\text{tot}/(N-1)$
with $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=5\Delta$ unless stated otherwise, so the total
donor--trap bias is fixed as $N$ grows and the nearest-neighbour mismatch is
$\dE=\varepsilon_\text{tot}/(N-1)$.
Disordered chains draw $\varepsilon_j\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$
independently.

\textit{Two-site model.}
For $N=2$ we use the spin-boson convention
$H=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\sigma_z+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\sigma_x$, i.e.\ an
inter-site coupling of $\Delta/2$; this is the convention used in
Sec.~\ref{sec:two_site} and Fig.~\ref{fig:two_site}.
The $N=2$ funnel of Sec.~\ref{sec:funnel} uses coupling $\Delta$ and therefore
gives a different enhancement ($2.75$ rather than $7.20$ at
$\varepsilon=5\Delta$).

\textit{Rates and grids.}
Unless stated otherwise, $\kappa=0.1\Delta$ and $\Gamma=0.01\Delta$.
One-dimensional sweeps use log-spaced $\gp\in[10^{-3},10^{3}]\Delta$ with
300 points for ordered chains (grid step $4.7\%$) and 100 points for disorder
ensembles (step $15\%$).
For the funnels we also refine $\gps$ with a bounded scalar optimizer; the
refined peak yields differ from the grid values by less than $10^{-4}$
(relative).

\textit{Enhancement ratio.}
We define $\etap=\max_{\gp}\eta(\gp)$ and $\gps$ its location.
The reference yield is $\etar=\eta(10^{-3}\Delta)$, the lowest grid point,
unless explicitly marked as the true coherent limit $\eta(0)$;
Sec.~\ref{sec:funnel} shows that this choice matters.
A realization is counted as showing ENAQT when $\gps$ is an interior grid
point and $\enh>1.01$.

\subsection{\label{sec:two_site}Two sites: the role of bias and trap rate}

Figure~\ref{fig:two_site}(a) and Table~\ref{tab:two_site} show the two-site
yield.
Without bias the yield decreases monotonically with $\gp$: coherent transfer
between degenerate sites is already efficient and dephasing only slows it.
With bias, the coherent yield drops (the excitation oscillates with reduced
amplitude $\propto\Delta^2/(\Delta^2+\varepsilon^2)$) while dephasing-assisted
hopping restores it, and the interior maximum sits close to the HSR resonance
$\gp=\varepsilon$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:hsr_rate}): the refined ratio
$\gps/\varepsilon$ rises from $0.88$ at $\varepsilon=0.5\Delta$ to $0.99$ at
$\varepsilon=5\Delta$, approaching one as the bias grows relative to the
coupling, as in the two-site result of Ref.~\cite{jacob2024} and the
line-broadening picture of Ref.~\cite{plenio2008}.
At very strong dephasing all curves collapse onto the Zeno tail,
$\eta(10^{3}\Delta)=0.043$.

\subsection{\label{sec:funnel}Linear energy funnels}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Figure~\ref{fig:funnel} and Table~\ref{tab:funnel} summarize funnels of
$N=2$--$20$ sites at fixed total bias $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=5\Delta$.
Every funnel shows an interior yield maximum.

\textit{The mismatch rule.}
The optimal dephasing rate equals the nearest-neighbour mismatch,
\begin{equation}
  \gps \simeq \dE = \frac{\varepsilon_\text{tot}}{N-1},
  \label{eq:mismatch_rule}
\end{equation}
with the refined $\gps/\dE$ between $0.988$ and $1.018$ for $N=2$--$20$
(grid values $0.975$--$1.032$; Fig.~\ref{fig:funnel}(c)).
Because every bond of a linear funnel has the same mismatch,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:hsr_rate}) is maximized on all bonds simultaneously at
$\gp=\dE$, which is also the Bloch frequency of the tilted chain.
This is the rule derived by Jacob \textit{et al.}\ for the boundary-driven
current, $\gamma_\text{max}\simeq U$ with $U$ the nearest-neighbour energy
difference~\cite{jacob2024}, here confirmed quantitatively for the trapping
yield of chains with uniform loss; Moix \textit{et al.}\ used the same
Lorentzian rate to predict an optimal dephasing equal to the disorder width
for diffusion in disordered chains~\cite{moix2013}.
A power-law fit over the same range gives $\gps\approx9.2\,\Delta\,N^{-1.20}$
($R^2=0.992$), but this exponent only describes the curvature of
$\varepsilon_\text{tot}/(N-1)$ on a log-log plot over $N=2$--$20$ (a power
fit of the mismatch itself gives $-1.22$); it carries no separate physics.

\textit{Peak yield, bias symmetry and the flat chain.}
The peak yield falls steadily, from $0.80$ at $N=2$ to $0.25$ at $N=20$,
because the transit time grows with $N$ while the loss rate $\Gamma$ is fixed.
Reversing the funnel into an uphill ramp leaves every yield unchanged, as
required by Sec.~\ref{sec:symmetry}.
The flat chain ($\varepsilon_j=0$) shows no ENAQT: its yield decreases
monotonically with $\gp$ at the default rates and over
$\kappa=0.01$--$100\,\Delta$ and $\Gamma=10^{-3}$--$10^{-1}\Delta$ for
$N=3$--$7$.
This is the end-to-end geometry, with the excitation starting at the end
opposite the trap, for which dephasing was found numerically not to help by
Plenio and Huelga~\cite{plenio2008} and explained by Kassal and
Aspuru-Guzik in the weak trapping and loss limit~\cite{kassal2012}; in other
source--trap geometries ordered systems can show ENAQT~\cite{kassal2012}.
Its coherent yield is nevertheless higher than the funnel's peak yield at
every length we checked: $0.773$ versus $0.722$ at $N=3$, $0.625$ versus
$0.518$ at $N=7$, and $0.461$ versus $0.320$ at $N=15$.
In this model, then, dephasing does not give the funnel a transport advantage;
it recovers part of the yield that the energy mismatch removes.
A downhill preference would require thermal relaxation, which pure dephasing
does not contain.

\textit{The enhancement ratio.}
Measured against $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$ the ratio grows almost linearly for
$N\le10$,
\begin{equation}
  \enh \approx 3.85\,N - 4.9\qquad (N\le 10,\;R^2=0.996),
\end{equation}
then plateaus at $36$--$38$ for $N=12$--$20$.
Measured against the true coherent limit, the same data give
$\etap/\eta(0)=107$ at $N=10$, $407$ at $N=15$ and $1.3\times10^{3}$
at $N=20$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:funnel}(b)).
The plateau therefore does not reflect a limit on noise-assisted transport;
it appears because, in long funnels, even $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$ already lifts the
yield well above its coherent value, by a factor of $3.3$ at $N=10$, $10.7$
at $N=15$ and $35$ at $N=20$.

\textit{Robustness.}
We repeated the funnel scan on a $3\times3$ grid of trap and loss rates,
$\kappa\in\{0.01,0.1,1\}\Delta$ and $\Gamma\in\{10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}\Delta$.
The mismatch rule survives with $\gps/\dE=0.85$--$1.09$ over all nine
combinations; the largest deviations occur for the fast trap $\kappa=\Delta$,
which broadens the last site and shifts $\gps$ downward.
The enhancement ratio does not: at $N=20$ it ranges from $4.7$
($\kappa=\Delta$, $\Gamma=10^{-3}\Delta$) to $91$ ($\kappa=0.01\Delta$,
$\Gamma=0.1\Delta$) against $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$.
The plateau also depends on the total bias.
For $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=2\Delta$, below the bandwidth $4\Delta$, the ratio
is only $1.53$--$1.66$ for $N=5$--$20$ ($1.60$--$1.73$ against $\eta(0)$),
whereas for $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=8\Delta$ it reaches $843$ against
$10^{-3}\Delta$ and $6.3\times10^{8}$ against $\eta(0)$ at $N=20$.
A natural reading is Wannier--Stark localization~\cite{wannier1960}: the
eigenstates of a chain with field step $\dE$ extend over about
$4\Delta/\dE=4(N-1)\Delta/\varepsilon_\text{tot}$ sites, which covers the
whole chain only when $\varepsilon_\text{tot}\lesssim4\Delta$.
Above that bias coherent transfer from site 1 to the trap is blocked and the
baseline collapses; below it the baseline stays finite and the ratio is
modest.
The value $36$--$38$ is therefore a property of $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=5\Delta$
and the default rates, not of funnels in general.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\label{sec:disorder}Random site disorder}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

We drew 100 disorder realizations with $\sigma=2\Delta$ for each
$N\in\{2,3,4,5,6,7,8,10,12,15\}$, 1{,}000 chains in total; the same seeds
($0$--$99$) are used at every $N$.
Table~\ref{tab:disorder} and Fig.~\ref{fig:disorder} give the statistics;
confidence intervals (CI) for medians are $95\%$ bootstrap intervals from
2{,}000 resamples.

\textit{Prevalence.}
An interior maximum with at least $1\%$ gain occurs in 974 of the 1{,}000
chains.
The fraction is lowest for the shortest chains ($89\%$ at $N=2$, $92\%$ at
$N=3$), where a realization can have a small bias and behave like the
degenerate case of Sec.~\ref{sec:two_site}, and is $98$--$100\%$ for
$N\ge4$.
So ENAQT is the typical behaviour of a disordered chain with a trap, not a
fine-tuned one, in line with earlier
studies~\cite{moix2013,novo2016,zhang2017,zerahharush2020,coates2021}.

\textit{Ratio versus yield.}
The median ratio grows quickly with $N$ and exceeds the ordered funnel's ratio
(against $\gp=10^{-3}\Delta$) from $N=10$ on: $35.7$ (CI $21.6$--$48.1$)
versus $32.4$ at $N=10$, and $244$ (CI $163$--$426$) versus $37.9$ at $N=15$.
The distribution is strongly right-skewed (mean $6.9\times10^{3}$ at $N=15$,
maximum $2.4\times10^{5}$).
Figure~\ref{fig:disorder}(b) shows where the large ratios come from: the median
peak yield decreases gently with $N$, from $0.82$ to $0.13$, while the median
baseline collapses from $0.66$ to $5.5\times10^{-4}$.
This is the baseline effect behind the early observation that the average
improvement is larger the more static disorder there
is~\cite{rebentrost2009}.
At this disorder strength the median peak yield is slightly above the funnel's
for $N=2$ and $3$ ($0.82$ versus $0.80$, $0.74$ versus $0.72$) and below it
for $N\ge4$ ($0.43$ versus $0.52$ at $N=7$; $0.13$ versus $0.32$ at $N=15$).
Measured against the true coherent limit the funnel's ratio is the larger one
at $N=10$ and $15$ ($107$ versus a median of $41$, and $407$ versus $354$).
Against the flat chain the comparison is one-sided: only 4 of the 1{,}000
disordered chains, all at $N=3$, reach a higher peak yield than the flat chain
of the same length.

\textit{Matched disorder.}
The comparison with the funnel depends on the disorder strength.
Choosing $\sigma=\dE/\sqrt{2}$, so that the root-mean-square difference
between neighbouring site energies equals the funnel's mismatch
$\dE=5\Delta/(N-1)$, the median peak yield (50 realizations per $N$) exceeds
the funnel's at every length tested: $0.683$ versus $0.658$ at $N=4$,
$0.556$ versus $0.518$ at $N=7$, $0.479$ versus $0.424$ at $N=10$ and
$0.418$ versus $0.320$ at $N=15$.
A random landscape with the same typical step as the funnel, but no net
bias, transports better than the funnel, consistent with the bias symmetry of
Sec.~\ref{sec:symmetry}: the net drop of the funnel is a cost, not a help.

\textit{Disorder strength.}
At $N=7$ (50 realizations per $\sigma$) the median ratio is $1.00$ at
$\sigma=0.25\Delta$, $1.74$ at $\sigma=\Delta$, $8.3$ at $2\Delta$, $97$ at
$3\Delta$ and $7.2\times10^{3}$ at $5\Delta$, while the median peak yield
falls from $0.60$ to $0.26$.
For $\sigma\ge\Delta$ the median ratio is well described by
$\ln(\enh)\approx2.15\,\sigma/\Delta-1.83$ ($R^2=0.996$;
Fig.~\ref{fig:disorder}(c)).
We do not attach a mechanism to this exponential form: it describes the range
studied, and the baseline it reflects is set jointly by localization, the
trap and the loss rate.
For orientation, the band-centre localization length of the infinite chain,
from a transfer-matrix calculation with $4\times10^{5}$ sites, is
$\xi=139$ sites at $\sigma=0.25\Delta$, $35$ at $0.5\Delta$, $9.2$ at
$\Delta$, $4.4$ at $1.5\Delta$, $2.8$ at $2\Delta$ and $1.6$ at $3\Delta$.
At weak disorder this follows $\xi\approx8.75\,\Delta^2/\sigma^2$, the
Gaussian equivalent of the band-centre result $105\,\Delta^2/W^2$ for box
disorder of width $W$~\cite{kappus1981}.
The $N=7$ chains are thus shorter than $\xi$ for $\sigma\le\Delta$ and several
localization lengths long for $\sigma\ge2\Delta$ ($7/\xi\approx2.5$ at
$2\Delta$).

\subsection{\label{sec:optimal}Optimizing the ratio}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

To see what the ratio rewards, we maximized $\enh$ over the site energies
$\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_{N-1}$, each restricted to
$[-5\Delta,5\Delta]$, with $\varepsilon_N=0$.
We used the differential-evolution routine of SciPy (one run with seed 42,
population $8(N-1)$, 100 generations, L-BFGS-B polish), with $\etap$ taken
over 30 log-spaced points in $[10^{-3},10^{2}]\Delta$ and
$\etar=\max[\eta(10^{-8}\Delta),10^{-12}]$.

\textit{What the optimizer returns.}
For $N=5$, $7$ and $10$ the result is a two-level step at the bounds: the
first $\lfloor(N-1)/2\rfloor$ sites at $+5\Delta$, the following
$\lfloor(N-1)/2\rfloor$ at $-5\Delta$, and the trap at $0$ (for $N=10$ the
ninth site sits at $-0.28\Delta$; Fig.~\ref{fig:optimal}(a)).
For $N=3$ the profile is reversed, $(-5,+5,0)\Delta$.
For $N=12$ and $15$ the profiles are irregular; with a single run and no
convergence check we treat them as not converged.
These landscapes achieve very large ratios ($87$ at $N=3$, $9.0\times10^{3}$ at
$N=5$, $8.0\times10^{5}$ at $N=7$) by making coherent transfer almost
impossible.

\textit{The objective has no finite optimum.}
Fixing the step profile and varying its height $B$ shows why the optimizer ends
at the bounds (Table~\ref{tab:steps}): the ratio grows without limit as $B$
increases, by orders of magnitude per doubling, while the peak yield falls.
The returned profile is the corner of the search box, not a property of the
transport problem.

\textit{Comparison.}
The optimized peak yields are below those of simple landscapes that lie inside
the same search box: $0.65$, $0.32$ and $0.21$ at $N=3$, $7$ and $10$, against
$0.72$, $0.52$ and $0.42$ for the $\varepsilon_\text{tot}=5\Delta$ funnel and
$0.77$, $0.62$ and $0.55$ for the flat chain.
Figure~\ref{fig:optimal}(b) also shows a unit-step funnel,
$\varepsilon_j=(N-j)\Delta$, used in earlier versions of this work; for
$N>6$ it extends beyond the box and has a larger total bias, so it is a
reference curve rather than a competitor.

\textit{Numerical limits.}
The baseline proxy $\eta(10^{-8}\Delta)$ agrees with $\eta(0)$ to $0.2\%$ for
the optimized $N=7$ profile but overestimates it by $16\%$ at $N=10$
($3.06\times10^{-10}$ versus $2.64\times10^{-10}$), and for $N=12$ and $15$
the baseline approaches the $10^{-12}$ floor.
Ratios for $N\ge10$ in this section are therefore indicative only.
Maximizing the enhancement ratio selects for a poor coherent baseline, not for
good transport, and it should not be used as a design objective.
Optimizing the absolute flux instead, as Lawrence \textit{et al.}\ did with
gradient methods~\cite{lawrence2026}, gives nearly flat landscapes for
short-range tunnelling, consistent with the flat-chain result of
Sec.~\ref{sec:funnel}.

\subsection{\label{sec:fmo}FMO benchmark}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

For the seven-site FMO Hamiltonian of Adolphs and Renger~\cite{adolphs2006}
(site energies and couplings in cm$^{-1}$, expressed in units of
$\Delta_\text{FMO}=87.7\,\text{cm}^{-1}$, the BChl~1--2 coupling), with the
excitation starting on BChl~1 and the trap on BChl~3, which faces the
reaction center (Adolphs and Renger proposed BChls~3 and 4 as the linker
pigments~\cite{adolphs2006}; see also Ref.~\cite{rebentrost2009}), we find
\begin{equation*}
  \gps = 1.87\,\Delta_\text{FMO},\qquad
  \etap = 0.46,\qquad
  \eta(0)=0.098,\qquad
  \etap/\eta(0) = 4.7 .
\end{equation*}
In time units, $\hbar/\Delta_\text{FMO}=60.5$\,fs, so the default rates
correspond to $\kappa^{-1}=0.61$\,ps and $\Gamma^{-1}=6.1$\,ps.

\textit{Dephasing rate.}
For the Drude bath of Ishizaki and Fleming~\cite{ishizaki2009}
($\lambda=35\,\text{cm}^{-1}$, $\gamma_c^{-1}=50$\,fs, $T=300$\,K), the
high-temperature pure-dephasing rate of a single site energy is
$2\lambda k_BT/\hbar\gamma_c=1.57\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$.
With independent baths on each site a site-to-site coherence decays at twice
this rate, $4\lambda k_BT/\hbar\gamma_c=3.1\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$, which is the
quantity that corresponds to $\gp$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:lindblad}).
The estimate scales with $\gamma_c^{-1}$; a $100$\,fs relaxation time, also
attributed to Ref.~\cite{ishizaki2009} in the literature, would double it.
The optimum $\gps=1.9\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$ is thus of the same order as the
estimate (a factor $1.7$ below it for $50$\,fs), consistent with earlier
conclusions that FMO operates near the ENAQT
optimum~\cite{rebentrost2009,caruso2009} and with the optimal dephasing
observed in a photonic FMO simulation~\cite{tang2024}.

\textit{Rates.}
The size of the enhancement depends on the fast loss of the default model.
With $\kappa^{-1}=1$\,ps and $\Gamma^{-1}=1$\,ns, closer to the biological
values, the coherent yield is already $0.87$ and the enhancement is $1.14$
($\etap=0.99$ at $\gps=1.78\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$); with $\kappa^{-1}=0.25$\,ps
it is $1.04$ ($\gps=1.71\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$).
The optimal rate is stable, the ratio is not; a small gain at realistic
parameters is also the conclusion of Ref.~\cite{zerahharush2021}.

\textit{Trap site.}
An earlier version of this work placed the trap on BChl~7; that choice gives a
much larger ratio ($32$ against $10^{-3}\Delta_\text{FMO}$, $39$ against
$\eta(0)$, at $\gps=1.55\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$) with a similar peak yield
($0.44$).
The difference lies in the coherent baseline: the long-time average
population that coherent evolution from BChl~1 places on the trap site, without
trap or loss, is $0.013$ for BChl~3 but only $0.0024$ for BChl~7.

\subsection{\label{sec:2d}Square lattices}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

As a first look beyond one dimension we computed $L\times L$ square lattices
($L=2$--$6$) with uniform coupling $\Delta$, a diagonal energy gradient from
$5\Delta$ at the source corner to $0$ at the trap corner, and the same rates
(80 log-spaced $\gp$ points in $[10^{-3},10^{2}]\Delta$,
$\etar=\eta(10^{-8}\Delta)$).
Table~\ref{tab:2d} lists the results.
The mismatch rule carries over: each step along a shortest path lowers the
energy by $\dE=5\Delta/[2(L-1)]$, and $\gps$ agrees with this to within $5\%$
($2.62$ versus $2.50$ at $L=2$, $0.53$ versus $0.50$ at $L=6$).
The enhancement stays modest ($3.1$--$4.7$) and the peak yield falls with
lattice size as in 1D.
For disordered lattices ($\sigma=2\Delta$ on all sites except the trap, which
stays at $0$; 50 realizations per $L$; 50 $\gp$ points) $76$--$96\%$ of
realizations have $\enh>1.01$.
Unlike the 1D criterion, this count does not require an interior maximum.
Larger ensembles and other geometries are needed before drawing conclusions
about dimensionality.

\section{\label{sec:discussion}Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\textit{What is robust.}
In ordered funnels and square lattices the optimal dephasing rate equals the
nearest-neighbour energy mismatch, the HSR resonance applied to every bond,
and this survives two-decade changes of the trap and loss rates.
The peak yield decreases smoothly with length.
Both quantities are directly comparable with experiment, where the yield and
the dephasing rate are the measurable quantities~\cite{maier2019}.

\textit{What dephasing does here.}
Because pure dephasing is an infinite-temperature bath, it cannot favour
downhill transfer, and the yield is the same for a funnel and its reverse.
The flat chain, which has no ENAQT at all, transports better than every funnel,
and weak random disorder with the funnel's typical step beats the funnel too.
Within this model ENAQT is best read as dephasing repairing the damage done by
energy mismatch.
Whether a real energy landscape gains from dephasing beyond that depends on
physics the model lacks, chiefly thermal relaxation toward lower sites.

\textit{What is not robust.}
The enhancement ratio mixes two different quantities: how well
dephasing-assisted transport works, and how badly coherent transport fails.
In long, strongly biased or disordered chains the second dominates.
As a result the ratio (i) has no unique length dependence, since it can
plateau or grow by orders of magnitude depending on the reference rate, the
total bias and the loss rate; (ii)~is largest for disordered chains whose
absolute yield is worse; (iii)~when optimized, has no finite maximum and
selects landscapes that block coherent transfer; and (iv)~for FMO falls from
$4.7$ to $1.14$ when the trap and loss rates are made biological.
We therefore recommend reporting $\etap$ and $\gps$, with the ratio given
only together with the exact reference point and rates used.

\textit{Relation to earlier work.}
The mismatch rule follows from the HSR rate~\cite{haken1973,moix2013} and
was derived for the boundary-driven current of tilted chains, including its
$1/L$ form at fixed total tilt, by Jacob \textit{et al.}~\cite{jacob2024};
related length laws exist for disordered and lossy
chains~\cite{zhang2017,coates2021,skalkin2025}.
Our contribution is to show that the same rule fixes the optimum of the
trapping yield to within $2\%$ for funnels of $N=2$--$20$, that it survives
two-decade changes of the trap and loss rates, and that it carries over to
square lattices; the exponent $-1.20$ of a power-law fit, which we reported
in earlier versions, adds nothing beyond it.
The near-universal presence of ENAQT in disordered chains agrees with
Refs.~\cite{moix2013,novo2016,zhang2017,zerahharush2020,coates2021}; our
ensemble adds length-resolved statistics with the peak yield and baseline
separated and a direct comparison with ordered and flat chains.
We found no earlier statement that the trapping yield is invariant under bias
reversal or that the flat chain beats every funnel under pure dephasing,
although both follow directly from the infinite-temperature character of the
bath~\cite{moix2013,jacob2024}.
The critique of the enhancement ratio builds on earlier remarks that
enhancement depends on the comparison regime~\cite{kassal2013} and on the
practice of optimizing absolute flux~\cite{coates2021,lawrence2026}.

\textit{Limitations.}
The model is Markovian with uniform, uncorrelated pure dephasing; it neglects
non-Markovian bath memory, which matters for FMO on timescales below a
picosecond~\cite{ishizaki2009,moix2011}, and thermal relaxation toward lower
sites, which the HSR dephasing term cannot describe and which would break the
bias symmetry; in this model the word funnel describes the shape of the
landscape, not a preferred direction.
Disorder ensembles are moderate in size (100 realizations per $N$ for
$\sigma=2\Delta$, 50 elsewhere), and the optimizer results rest on a single
run per $N$.
Appendix~\ref{app:heom} explains why we do not use the QD3SET-1 spin-boson data
as a validation of the trapping model.

% =============================================================================
\section{\label{sec:conclusion}Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

Within a minimal Lindblad model of a dephased chain with a trap:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The optimal dephasing rate equals the nearest-neighbour energy mismatch:
  $\gps\approx\varepsilon$ for two sites, $\gps=\varepsilon_\text{tot}/(N-1)$
  to within $2\%$ for funnels of $N=2$--$20$ sites, to within $15\%$ across
  two decades of trap and loss rates, and to within $5\%$ for square lattices.
\item The yield is invariant under bias reversal, and the flat chain beats
  every funnel; dephasing recovers yield lost to mismatch.
\item ENAQT occurs in $97.4\%$ of 1{,}000 disordered chains
  ($\sigma=2\Delta$, $N=2$--$15$).
\item The enhancement ratio is dominated by the coherent baseline: its
  plateau in funnels, its large values in disordered chains and the unbounded
  values reached by optimizing it all reflect a vanishing baseline, not better
  transport.
\item For FMO with the trap on BChl~3 the optimal dephasing rate,
  $1.9\,\Delta_\text{FMO}$, is of the order of a standard estimate of the
  protein dephasing rate, and is stable when the rates are made
  biological, while the enhancement falls to $1.14$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{\label{app:heom}Remarks on the QD3SET-1 spin-boson data}

An earlier version of this work compared the trapping model with the 1{,}000
numerically exact HEOM spin-boson trajectories of the QD3SET-1
dataset~\cite{ullah2023} ($\lambda=0.1$--$1.0\,\Delta$,
$\gamma_c=1$--$10\,\Delta$, $\beta=0.1$--$1.0\,\Delta^{-1}$,
$\varepsilon\in\{0,\Delta\}$, $\rho_{11}(0)=1$, $t\le20\,\Delta^{-1}$).
Binning all trajectories by the high-temperature estimate
$\gp=2\lambda/(\beta\gamma_c)$ gives a non-monotonic mean of
$\rho_{22}(t=20\,\Delta^{-1})$ for $\varepsilon=\Delta$, with a maximum of
$0.771$ at $\gp\approx0.13\Delta$ against $0.608$ at the lowest bin.
We no longer treat this as evidence for ENAQT or as a validation of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lindblad}), for three reasons.
First, there is no trap: $\rho_{22}$ at a fixed time measures relaxation toward
thermal equilibrium, not a trapping yield.
Second, the binned curve pools trajectories with different temperatures and
reorganization energies, so its shape partly reflects how the parameter grid
maps onto $\gp$.
Third, the HEOM bath includes thermal relaxation and memory that the pure
dephasing model lacks.
A quantitative test of the trapping model against exact dynamics would need
HEOM (or similar) simulations that include the trap, which we leave for future
work.
